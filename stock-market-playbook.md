# 🐘 The Elephant Playbook

### Share-market rules, decoded 

> Everything below is **educational, not financial advice**, and there are **no stock tips** here. Price levels are historical examples, not current signals.

---

## ⚡ TL;DR: if you read nothing else

| # | The rule | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 🛡️ **Never risk more than 2% of your total capital on one trade.** | Survive first. You can't win later if you're wiped out now. |
| 2 | 📏 **Get the stop-loss from the chart, *then* decide how many shares.** | Stop-loss is the only thing you control. |
| 3 | ⚖️ **Skip any trade where reward < 3× risk (1:3).** | At 1:3 you can be wrong 75% of the time and still break even. |

## 🎮 Level 0: Meet the elephants

The market is an **elephant farm**. There are many kinds of elephants (traders), but the two that matter are:

- 🐘 **Patient elephants:** their #1 skill is *money management*. Other skills matter, but none of them work without it.
- 🐘💨 **Impatient elephants:** they are the ones destined to fail.

---

## 💰 Level 1: Money management (the 2% rule)

### The rule
> **Never put more than 2% of your *total* capital at risk on any single trade.**

Note the word *risk*. It means **the loss if your stop-loss gets hit**, not the amount you invest.

### Example (stock ₹1,000, capital ₹10,000)

- Buy at ₹1,000, stop-loss at ₹800, so the risk is **₹200 per share**.
- 2% of ₹10,000 is **₹200**, so you buy **just 1 share**, even though ₹10,000 could buy 10.

| | 🐘 Patient | 🐘💨 Impatient |
|---|---|---|
| Shares bought | 1 | 10 |
| Loss if stop hits | ₹200 (**2%**) | ₹2,000 (**20%**) |
| After a 2nd losing trade | **4%** down | **40%** down |
| Mental state | Still calm | "The market is a casino!" |

```
Capital left after 2 losing trades (start = 100%)

Patient    ████████████████████████████████  96%
Impatient  ████████████████████                60%
```

### The 3rd trade is where it gets spicy
On trade #3, the stock dips a bit and the impatient elephant, still haunted by the last two losses, panics and sells. The stock then bounces back strongly. The patient elephant is still in the game, recovers the earlier losses, and profits. **Lesson:** staying alive long enough to catch the big winner is the whole game.

### How to size a trade: the correct order of operations

```
 ① Read the chart → where is my idea proven WRONG?   (stop-loss)
          ↓
 ② Risk per share = entry − stop
          ↓
 ③ Max loss in ₹ = 2% × total capital
          ↓
 ④ Quantity = max loss ÷ risk per share
          ↓
 ⑤ Enough cash? (keep 10–15% free)  → if not, buy fewer
          ↓
 ⑥ Target from chart → reward ÷ risk ≥ 3 ?  → if not, SKIP
```

**Worked examples:**
- Chart says a **₹5 stop**, and 2% of capital is ₹200, so buy **40 shares** (200 ÷ 5).
- ₹100 stock with a **₹2 stop**: the risk rule allows 100 shares, but only ₹9,000 cash is left, so buy **90**.

### ❌ The classic newbie mistake
Newbies follow "2%" but choose quantity first (*"I can afford 100 shares"*), then squeeze the stop to ₹2 to make the math work. The chart actually needs ₹5. The stock dips ₹3–4 (normal!), they get stopped out, and then it rallies without them. **The chart decides the stop. Your wallet doesn't.**

### Other money-management rules
- You can take **many trades at once**, as long as **each** one respects the 2% cap.
- With a *great* setup, even putting all your capital into one trade is okay, provided the loss is still ≤ 2%.
- **Keep 10–15% of capital free** for surprise opportunities. The market creates new ones every day.
- Anything can happen anytime (e.g., one tweet from a US president can shake the market). All you have is your stop-loss, so follow it religiously.

## 🎲 Level 2: Why ten small bets beat one big bet

Both elephants risk the same total (20%). Is there a difference? **Yes: probability.**

Assume zero skill: each trade is a coin flip (50% win, 50% lose).

```
Impatient (one 20% bet):    P(lose 20%) = 50%                    😬
Patient   (ten 2% bets):    P(lose 20%) = all 10 fail
                                        = (1/2)^10 = 1/1024
                                        = under 0.1%             😌
```

- The guiding slogan, borrowed from a Maratha war hero's famous line: *if we survive, we fight again.* Protect your capital and there's always a next trade.
- People who call the market "gambling" usually haven't grasped this.
- **Reality check:** you don't *have* to take ten trades. Beginners can't watch ten positions anyway, and one excellent setup is fine. The only fixed rule is **never more than 2% per trade**.

## 👻 Level 3: Mind management

Reading about a 20% loss is fun. **Living** through one isn't.

- When a trade drops 5%, 10%, 15%, "ghosts" start dancing in your head. Two things happen:
  1. You **exit before your stop** (panic), or
  2. You **move the stop lower** and hope (ego).

  Both turn small losses into big ones.
- Blunt truth: **if you can't tame the ghosts, you will lose.** Everyone gets them, unless you're a perfectly calm sage.
- **You can't kill the ghosts, but you *can* shrink them**, by keeping positions small:

```
Same 15% drop in the stock. Very different panic:

Impatient (big position): you see  −15%  👻👻👻👻👻👻👻👻👻👻
Patient   (2% rule):      you see  −1.5% 👻
```

- **Leave ego outside.** Institutions trade billions; you're small change. An exit signal is an exit signal.
- **Accidental wins are dangerous.** The impatient elephant *could* just as easily win twice, and win more than the patient one, since it bought more shares. But a win that comes from breaking the rules is luck, not skill. You think your chart-reading is great, your confidence inflates, you bet bigger, and eventually fall into a deep pit.
- Good money management makes good mind management *possible*.

## ⚖️ Level 4: Risk-reward (the 1:3 rule)

**Risk** = entry − stop. **Reward** = target − entry. Both come from the chart, never from your imagination.

> Example: entry ₹100, stop ₹95 (risk ₹5), target ₹120 (reward ₹20) → **1:4** ✅
> For 1:3 here, the target must be at least ₹115. Below that, **don't take the trade.**

### Why 1:3 is so powerful (the maths)
Take 1000 trades with risk ₹1 and reward ₹3:
- 750 fail → lose ₹750
- 250 win → gain ₹750
- **Net: ₹0.** You break even while being wrong **75% of the time.**

Extra maths (before costs):

| Reward : Risk | Break-even win rate | You can be wrong up to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 : 1 | 50% | 50% |
| 1 : 2 | 33% | 67% |
| **1 : 3** | **25%** | **75%** |
| 1 : 4 | 20% | 80% |

- **Why not accept 1:2?** Brokerage, STT and other charges hit you on both buy *and* sell, and you will make execution mistakes. The 1:3 margin absorbs them. For intraday trades, a looser ratio is considered acceptable.
- **No setup is 100% accurate.** Anyone claiming a 100% setup is bluffing. Even a **40%-accuracy setup** works if you hold risk-reward strictly. (The chance of its first four trades all failing is 12.96%, which is not tiny, so don't quit after two losses.)
- **No stock is your life partner.** There are thousands of stocks. If one doesn't fit 1:3, another will.
- The mantra: ***"It is simple, but not easy."***
- **Get-rich-quick? No.** Many people see the market as a shortcut to fast money. Enter without knowing all this and you'll mostly make *other* people rich: experienced traders and brokers.

## 🚩 Level 5: Why ~90% of people lose (8 red flags)

| # | Red flag | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bad money management** | Oversized trades eat the capital and kill your trust in the market. |
| 2 | **Bad mind management** | Money + mind management drive 80%+ of results. |
| 3 | **Subjective stops/targets** | They must be objective and *chart-based*. Stop too tight = many small losses; too far = a big loss. A day-trade target might be ½–¾%, but a positional target of "10–20%" can't be invented. |
| 4 | **Reward < 3× risk** | Costs and mistakes then turn the maths negative over time. |
| 5 | **Exiting early or moving the stop down** | Panic or ego. Both enlarge losses. |
| 6 | **Emotions and ego** | They cause *big losses + small profits*. Even 99 trades of ₹1 profit and one ₹200 loss = net loss. Accuracy % matters less than **how much you make when right vs lose when wrong.** A trader often cited says a good trader can be wrong 80% of the time and still profit. |
| 7 | **Trading against your own signal** | Buying while the market is falling, when your setup says otherwise. |
| 8 | **Averaging down** | See below 👇 |

### ☠️ The averaging-down trap
Investors buy more as a stock falls to "lower the average price". It's a major cause of investor wipe-outs.

- Example: 20 shares at ₹800 → buy 40 at ₹400 → buy 80 at ₹200... and the cycle continues.
- **Reliance Communications** was in the Nifty 50 until 2011, traded near ₹800 around 2008–09, and (by 2019) cost less than 60 paise. Suzlon and Jaypee Associates are other former "blue chips" that fell hard.
- **Rules:** average only *rising* stocks. For long-term holdings, use the **monthly chart** to decide when to exit. If it falls and gives an exit signal, leave. Re-enter only if it starts rising again.
- **Don't buy junk because it's cheap.** Think of a flat next door selling for ₹50 lakh when similar ones cost ₹2 crore: you'd first ask what's wrong with the title. With shares, many skip that question.

### 🌱 Investor corner
This isn't anti-investing. Examples of long-term multibaggers include Infosys (~90× in 21 years), TCS (~15× in 14 years), Motherson Sumi (~260× over 22 years to 2018) and HDFC Bank (~300× since 1995), plus Crisil and Ajanta Pharma, all *before* dividends. The point: buy **good, uptrending** stocks regularly and **exit at the right time**, and you could retire early.

> **Mini-fact:** Why does Infosys look "cheap" if it gave 90×? **Bonus issues and splits.** One pre-1994 Infosys share equals about 1,024 shares today. ₹5,000 invested in 1993 at today's ~₹700 price would be 1,024 × 700 ≈ **₹7,16,800**. Mechanics: a 1:1 bonus gives one extra share per share held, so your share count doubles and the price halves, and your holding's value doesn't change that day. Companies also split or issue bonuses to keep prices affordable (e.g., a ~₹70,000 stock like MRF would be ~₹700 after a 100-for-1 split, which suits far more people and lifts trading activity).

## 🔬 Level 6: Technical vs fundamental (the case for reading charts)

**Fundamental analysis** = predicting a company's future profit. The case against doing it solo:

```
        Fuel prices        Competitor pricing
              \                 /
 Job market ── ONE COMPANY'S PROFIT ── EV / new technology
              /                 \
        Taxes & GST          ₹ vs $ rate
        + management exits + hundreds more factors
```

- Every sector has *different* factors (a battery company's study won't help with a pharma stock).
- Balance sheets are **old** by the time you read them.
- News is usually **already priced in**, and insiders know first.
- **Example:** after the Idea-Vodafone merger news in October 2017, Idea jumped from ~₹45 to ~₹60 in 10 days (~33%) between 13 and 23 Oct. Volume was far above its 20-day average. Whether insider trading was involved is unknowable, but the *pattern* is visible on a chart without knowing why. What would a 31-March-2017 balance sheet have told a trader on 14 Oct 2017?
- Big moves are often **sentiment-driven**: a stock can rise 10–20% in a month even though a company's fundamentals can't change that fast. A positional trade in Idea during those 10 days would have paid well.

**Technical analysis** = read price and volume only. Whatever the news is, it shows up as buying or selling. The *same* method works for Maruti or TCS, with no sector expertise needed.

> ⚠️ This is one school of thought, not universal truth. Fundamental analysts deserve respect: let them do the hard work, and technical traders can "come eat the cream" once it shows up in price and volume.
>
> Other approaches also work for some people (e.g., VWAP-only trading, Heikin Ashi charts). No approach is 100% right or 100% wrong: those who make money make it in different ways, so pick what you can handle. The market's bottom line is simple: if you're right, take the money and go; if you're wrong, pay and go.

## 🕯️ Level 7: Charts and candlesticks 101

Stick to **plain candlestick charts** (skip Renko, Heikin Ashi and the like, to avoid confusion).

```
      |   ← upper wick = the HIGH
    ┌───┐
    │   │  ← body = OPEN to CLOSE
    │   │     🟢 green: close > open (price rose)
    └───┘     🔴 red:   close < open (price fell)
      |   ← lower wick = the LOW
```

- Market opens **9:15 am** and closes **3:30 pm** (375 minutes).
- The **closing price is calculated by a formula** from the last half-hour's trades, not simply the last trade.
- One candle = any time period: 1 min up to 1 month.
- **Avoid stocks whose price doesn't move every moment** (thinly traded ones). Trade only stocks that trade actively.

```
1 month  = ~22 daily candles
1 week   =  5 daily candles
1 day    = 25 candles of 15 minutes
```
*Example: SBI's weekly candle showed a low of ₹299.70 (Thursday) and a high of ₹323.70 (Friday), both visible inside its five daily candles.*

| Who you are | Typical chart |
|---|---|
| 🌳 Investor (holds months/years) | Monthly |
| 📈 Positional trader (days to weeks) | Weekly + daily |
| ☀️ Day trader (same-day) | 1-hour or 15-min |
| ⚡ Scalper (minutes; mostly big institutions) | 5-min or 1-min |

**Job-holders:** intraday on 15-min candles is hard to follow, but **daily and weekly candles give plenty of opportunities**. Starting while employed is possible, but it takes heavy time and effort.

## 🧱 Level 8: Support and resistance (the core skill)

Price falls when sellers are aggressive, and stops when buyers show up in force. That level is **support**. Price rises when buyers are aggressive, and stops when sellers swarm in. That's **resistance**.

### 📌 Case study: Tata Motors, daily chart, 2019

```
₹238.90  ═══ RESISTANCE: price hit it 18–19 Apr, sellers pushed it back
            (candle: tiny body, long wicks = "high-wave" candle)
   ⋮
₹195–197 ─── local resistance (target zone)
   ⋮
₹168.55  ═══ THE FLIP LEVEL
            • 27–28 Dec 2018, 28 Mar, 15 May: SUPPORT (buyers defended it)
            • then it broke, and on 1 Jul & 17 Jul: RESISTANCE
            • 6 Nov: big green candle + heavy volume broke it upward
            • 7–15 Nov: SUPPORT again
₹165     ✖ stop-loss (just under ₹165.55, the low of the next red candle)
   ⋮
₹106.40  ═══ SUPPORT (22 Aug and 4 Sep 2019)
```

### 🔄 Key insight: role reversal
> **Old support becomes new resistance, and old resistance becomes new support.** A break with a big candle and big volume makes the flip more likely to hold.

### 🎯 How to trade it
1. **Price near support + small, indecisive candles** (high-wave, doji, shooting-star types) = decent odds of a reversal.
2. **Wait for a green confirmation candle.** *Touching* support is not a signal.
3. **Stop-loss** just below the support structure: ₹165.
4. **Target** at the next local resistance: ₹195.
5. **Entry** = buy above the high of the confirmation candle.
6. **Check risk-reward before entering:**

| If the confirmation candle lets you buy at... | Risk | Reward | Ratio | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ₹175 | ₹10 | ₹20 | 1 : 2 | ❌ **Skip.** "I missed the bus." |
| ₹171–172 | ~₹6–7 | ~₹23–24 | ~1 : 3 | ✅ **Take it** |

> 🏹 **Hunter mindset:** No signal yet? Then no trade. Wait for the prey to come into range. Nobody is holding a gun to your head to trade today, and hundreds of other stocks exist.

**Reminder:** all of this is *probabilities*, not certainties. The stop-loss is your best friend.

## 📐 Level 9: Trends (Dow Theory in 60 seconds)

| Trend | Highs | Lows | Example sequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 📈 **Uptrend** | Higher highs (HH) | Higher lows (HL) | low 100 → high 120 → low 110 → high 140 → low 125 |
| 📉 **Downtrend** | Lower highs (LH) | Lower lows (LL) | high 150 → low 130 → high 140 → low 115 → high 125 |
| ↔️ **Sideways** | Roughly flat | Roughly flat | bouncing in a range |

- A green candle ≠ an uptrend. Don't buy long-term until a **resistance actually breaks**.
- **Trendlines** join highs or lows. A break *with big volume* matters.
- When two trendlines converge, you get a **triangle**. A break in either direction can produce a big move. Triangle breaks are rare, but when you catch one, the trade is usually very rewarding.

**Chart examples:**
- 🟢 **Kotak Mahindra Bank (monthly):** making HH and HL. ₹1,403 was a previous higher high and the latest higher low. If it breaks below, the uptrend is over.
- 🔴 **Eicher Motors (monthly):** HH in Sep 2017 → HL in Jan 2018 → *lower high* in Apr 2018 (the warning bell) → Sep 2018 broke the higher low, so a downtrend was confirmed.
- ↔️ **Reliance (monthly):** sideways from June 2009 to Jan 2017. It then broke out of a roughly 8-year trendline/triangle in Feb 2017 and rallied.

### Rules for long-term buying
✅ Buy only **uptrending** stocks, ideally near a **higher low** after a reversal sign.
❌ **Never** buy downtrends for the long term. **Don't touch** sideways stocks.
**Tata Motors long-term (as of 2019):** not a buy. A downtrend since 2017 with lower highs and lower lows. A big green candle at support in October (closer to a *morning star* than a bullish engulfing) is a good sign, but a reversal is confirmed only if ₹239 breaks. It may just be the first leg (an "A" wave) of an A-B-C correction, with a "B" wave pulling price lower again. Fine for a short swing; for long-term investing, think ten times.

## 🚪 Level 10: Entries and exits

### 🅰️ Long-term exit: the Eicher case study
Eicher was a huge multibagger, but from its top (~₹33,460 in Sep 2017) it fell about **50% to ~₹16,000**. The point: *don't buy and forget for years.*

```
Sep 2017  ● Peak ~₹33,460 with a "high-wave" candle (small body, long
            wicks) = uncertainty → be ready to exit
Oct 2017  ● small green candle
Nov 2017  ● big red candle swallows it (bearish engulfing)
            → bearish → ideal exit zone
Jan 2018  ● breaks previous higher low → next low will be a LOWER low
Feb–Apr   ● three green candles... but fail to cross the Sep 2017 high
            → weak price action. Don't re-enter!
Jun 2018  ● breaks higher low again → exit (~₹29,800, not the top!)
Sep 2018  ● downtrend confirmed
            ↓
          ~₹16,000 = about half the peak
```

**Rules:** exit on a monthly-chart signal. Don't hope. **Re-enter only when price makes a new high** (here: crossing the Sep 2017 peak). One or two green candles aren't enough. Earlier history agrees: the Jul–Aug 2015 candles also gave an exit signal, and re-entry was fine once an Aug 2016 green candle broke the 2015 high. The Nov 2016 big red candle did *not* make a lower low and had a long lower wick (rejection), so it was not an exit signal.

### 🅱️ Swing trade: the double bottom (HDFC Bank, daily, 2019)
*Swing trade* = buy near support, sell at resistance.

```
Support ₹1,082 tested: 5–7 Aug, then 23–24 Aug
Dip low in between: ₹1,069  → price came back up
17–18 Sep: price returns to support → 19 Sep: buy
Stop: just below ₹1,069      Target: ₹1,145 (local resistance)
Even buying as high as ₹1,088 → risk 19, reward 57 → 1:3 ✅
```

- Target hit the very next day (that's rare).
- **Don't exit just because the target hit.** Stay until a reversal sign appears, and **trail your stop** from ₹1,069 up to ₹1,145. Profit is now locked in, and whatever comes after is a bonus.
- A second swing setup is the **trendline breakout**. Double *tops* aren't useful for delivery trading because you can't hold a positional short in cash.

## 🤖 Level 11: Orders, algos, and stop-hunts

### How a trade actually happens
- The **order book** lists who wants to buy/sell at which prices. Retail traders usually see the top 5 levels; institutions reportedly see far more.
- **Bid-ask spread** = gap between the best buy price and the best sell price (e.g., ₹99.95 vs ₹100.05 = 10 paise).
- A trade happens when a buyer pays up, a seller accepts less, or a new order crosses the gap.

| Order type | What it does | Guaranteed to fill? |
|---|---|---|
| **Market** | Buys/sells right now at the best price available | ✅ Yes |
| **Limit** | Buys at your price or lower (sells at your price or higher) | ❌ No |
| **Stop-loss** | Triggers a sell if price falls to a level (or a buy if it rises to one) | ❌ No |

Example use: set a **stop-loss buy** just above a trendline to catch a breakout.

### The stop-hunt problem (the theory)
Big institutions run algorithms and can see the order book. If they hold a stock and want to buy more cheaply, they may push price down to where many retail stop-losses sit (say ₹165), triggering a flood of sell orders they can buy. That's why you see candles with **long wicks** that tag a stop and snap back.

**The workaround:**
- **Don't place the stop-loss order in the system.** Instead, keep it in your head and follow it strictly.
- Wait for the day to **close**. If it closes below the stop, exit the next day.
- **Trade-off:** if price crashes through, you might exit lower than planned. The observation is that avoiding false stop-outs helps more often than slippage hurts, and the 1:3 buffer accounts for it.
- Retail "algo" services exist, but an algorithm can't be much better than the trader who programs it, and some chart patterns can only be recognised by eye. A machine may place orders faster, but the human brain still thinks better.

## 🎒 Level 12: The starter pack (beginner roadmap)

```
 1. Learn the rules first (money + mind management)
          ↓
 2. Pick 5–10 big, liquid stocks (top ~10–12 by market cap)
    e.g. Reliance, HDFC Bank, TCS, HUL
          ↓
 3. Monthly chart: draw support/resistance + trendlines
          ↓
 4. Weekly/daily charts: mark local levels
          ↓
 5. Once every evening: scan the charts
          ↓
 6. Stock near support + small candles? → wait for confirmation candle
          ↓
 7. Check: stop-loss ✓  target ✓  risk-reward ≥ 1:3 ✓  risk ≤ 2% ✓
          ↓
 8. Enter → follow the stop religiously
          ↓
 9. Review: did I follow my rules? (not "did I profit?")
```

**Do:**
- **Use one setup at a time**, and give it time. If two trades fail, don't jump to a new setup.
- **Make sure every condition is met** before entering. Entering early on a hunch ("it'll probably signal soon") raises the odds of losing.
- **Keep indicators few:** price action first, then moving averages, MACD, RSI/Stochastic, Bollinger Bands. A practical toolkit: simple candles, a few patterns, price action, Bollinger, RSI, MACD and DMI. Pivot points aren't part of it.
- **Use free charting:** free accounts on investing.com give charts on any timeframe.
- **Start with ~5 shares**, scale up slowly.
- **Expect early mistakes**, such as entering before the reversal arrives because "it will surely bounce from support." Entries get fine-tuned with practice.
- Read **Murphy, Pring, and Edwards & Magee**. The old masters (Dow, Elliott, Wyckoff) drew charts by hand on graph paper, so you're lucky.

**Don't:**
- ❌ Jump into **futures and options** early. Move to futures only when the margin required is just slightly more than what the same trade would cost in cash.
- ❌ Start with **volatile stocks** (long wicks on both sides). Example: Hindalco's daily chart. Bank Nifty is also volatile for a beginner.
- ❌ Take a trade daily. No trade = fine.
- ❌ Believe the **RSI myth:** RSI above ~60 doesn't automatically mean "overbought, sell." HDFC Bank rose about 12.5× in 12 years with RSI mostly above 60.
- ❌ Compare yourself to a mutual fund in your first years. Early losses are the tuition fee.

## 📊 Level 13: How to keep score

Don't measure by monthly or yearly returns. Some positions last about three weeks, so measure **return per trade** and, more importantly, **discipline**.

> **Discipline is the scoreboard:** if you followed your rules and lost, no regret. If you broke your rules and won, that win is *bad*. It teaches the wrong lesson.

The closing philosophy:
- *Keep it simple.* Don't get trapped in big words, and do only what you can handle. Never break discipline.
- **Respect the market:** nobody can know more than the market. Steer clear of anyone who claims to know everything or to be 100% right.
- **Expect to take hits** in the first 2–3 years. That's how you learn to respect money management, mind management, and risk-reward.
- In a falling market, the suggestion is: no need to fear. Short positions can be taken, because bear markets offer as much opportunity as bull markets. (Positional shorts aren't possible in the cash segment.)

## ⚠️ Reality check (read before you act)

- This playbook reflects **one school of thought** (discretionary technical trading) from a 2019 thread, and no track record is shown. All price levels (Tata Motors, HDFC Bank, Eicher, etc.) are historical examples, **not current signals**.
- The rule is 2% risk **per trade**, with many open trades allowed. Ten open trades at 2% = up to **20% of capital at risk** at once. Many traders also cap *total* open risk (a common range is 5–6%) and watch for stocks that move together (same sector), since they can all hit stops on the same bad day.
- Gaps can jump past a stop, so a "2% risk" can become a bigger loss.
- The **stop-hunt theory** and the **"fundamentals aren't worth it"** stance are debated among traders. Treat them as views, not facts.
- Shorting positionally needs derivatives, which the same playbook says beginners should avoid at first.
- **This is educational, not financial advice.**
